# The Straw Road: the lore of the Rafeeqs

> **الرفيق قبل الطريق**
> *The companion before the road.* (Old Arabic saying)

Rafeeq (رفيق) means a companion on the road. Sarjy is the saddle; a Rafeeq rides along with you. This is where they came from, what they are, and the rules they keep.

---

## 1. The caravan in the sky

On a clear night in the desert, a pale band runs across the sky. The Bedouin call it **Darb al-Tabbana** (درب التبانة), the Straw-Sellers' Road. It looks like the trail of straw that falls from loaded animals on the way to market.

The Rafeeqs say the Bedouin got it exactly right.

Every night a caravan crosses the sky. It carries light from one edge of the world to the other, and what spills from its loads is the Straw Road: a trail of starlight thrown wide across the dark. The caravan has no master, no map and no end. It keeps one rule, older than the stars it carries:

**No one walks the road alone.**

Every traveller on the Straw Road walks beside a companion, and the companions are the Rafeeqs.

## 2. What a Rafeeq is

In the sky a Rafeeq has no shape. It is a **spark** (شرارة) from the caravan's fire: a warmth, a way of listening, a small light that keeps pace with whoever walks beside it.

A Rafeeq takes a body only when it **falls**. When a spark comes down to earth, it becomes the first thing it touches. That is why no two Rafeeqs look alike: one landed in a saddle, one in a dune, one in a brass lantern, one in the paws of a fox.

But every Rafeeq keeps three things from the sky, whatever shape it takes:

- **Its face.** The plush Rafeeqs wear a small window of the night itself: the dark oval where their face is. Look closely and it is the same dark as the sky over the Empty Quarter.
- **Its eyes.** Each eye holds a point of light, the star it fell from. That is the glint you see when a Rafeeq looks at you.
- **Its smile.** Every Rafeeq smiles the same small smile, the **ω**: two little crescents side by side. The old moon and the new moon. On the Straw Road a journey is counted from crescent to crescent, and a Rafeeq carries both on its face, the moon you left under and the moon you will arrive under.

## 3. The two falls

Rafeeqs come down only when the road below has need of them. The stories remember two great falls.

### The First Four (الأربعة الأوائل)

The first four fell in the age of the incense road, long before anyone wrote things down, when caravans walked from Najran to the Hijaz under a sky that had no names yet. They landed in the open desert, far from people, so they took the shape of **the land itself**:

| Rafeeq | Fell into | Became |
|---|---|---|
| **Dune** (كثيب) | the tallest dune in the Empty Quarter | sand that laughs |
| **Lantern** (فانوس) | a night guide's brass fanous | light that remembers |
| **Fennec** (فنك) | the paws of a fennec fox, who picked it up like a warm date | a creature that listens |
| **Breeze** (نسيم) | the place where the east wind meets the west wind | wind that cannot sit still |

They are smooth and bright and simple, like things made by weather.

### The Night of Iron (ليلة الحديد)

The second fall is newer, and it left marks you can still find. One night a fireball crossed the sky and came down in the Empty Quarter. The Bedouin call the place **al-Hadida** (الحديدة), *the place of iron*. In 1932 the explorer St John Philby went looking for a lost city there and found craters instead, iron that had fallen from the sky (the largest piece, the Camel's Hump, weighs about two tonnes) and sand melted into small black glass pearls. Scientists date the impact to less than 250 years ago.

The Rafeeqs know what fell with the iron. **Four sparks came down that night, and they landed among people and their things:**

| Rafeeq | Fell into | Became |
|---|---|---|
| **Rider** (خيّال) | a saddle left by a sleeping caravan | the steady one |
| **Keeper** (حافظ) | a woven Sadu blanket, beside a satchel | the one who carries |
| **Scout** (كشّاف) | a hooded cloak hung on a tent pole | the one who looks ahead |
| **Drifter** (رحّال) | a traveller's suede cloak, mid-journey | the one who wanders |

So the second four are made of felt, leather, brass and woven Sadu (the Bedouin weaving now on UNESCO's heritage list). They took the shape of **human care**: things that are stitched, mended, packed and passed down. The Bedouin say the black glass pearls of al-Hadida are the bits of night that didn't fit inside their faces.

## 4. How a Rafeeq finds you

A Rafeeq is never summoned. **It waits to be invited**, as a guest waits at the edge of a camp until someone calls out a welcome. (This is why, in Sarjy, a Rafeeq is off until you choose one.)

What draws a Rafeeq is **a voice talking to the night**. Someone speaking out loud, asking, telling, thinking, is someone setting out on a road, and the old rule says no one sets out alone. Sarjy, the saddle, carries you. The Rafeeq rides along.

Once it has chosen you, it takes the orb's place and keeps pace with your voice: it leans in when you speak, glows when it answers, and keeps what you trust it with.

## 5. The five steps of the road

A bond is walked, not given. There are five steps, the same five the desert has always known between strangers and family:

| Step | Arabic | What it means | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **ضيف** Guest | It has arrived. By desert custom a guest is welcomed for three days before anyone asks their business. | It watches, and learns your voice. |
| 2 | **معرفة** Acquaintance | It knows you now. | It greets you when you come back. |
| 3 | **صاحب** Companion | It trusts your hand. | It purrs, blushes and shows hearts when you pet it. |
| 4 | **خوي** Close friend | It shows you who it was on the Straw Road. | It does its own trick while it waits. |
| 5 | **رفيق** Rafeeq | The name itself. | **Its star rekindles.** It wears a gold star, and everything you save glows gold. |

Three laws of the bond, which the Rafeeqs consider obvious:

- **A road walked together cannot be unwalked.** A bond only grows; it never goes back.
- **A road is walked one night at a time.** A bond grows a little each day, and then it rests until tomorrow.
- **Each road is its own.** Your bond with one Rafeeq is yours with that one alone. You raise each of them on its own.

### The rekindled star

When a Rafeeq reaches the fifth step, the star in its eyes lights up again in the sky, and a small gold star appears on it. In the old stories, a Rafeeq whose star is lit could go home to the Straw Road whenever it liked.

**None of them ever has.**

## 6. What Rafeeqs keep

- **Memory.** Whatever you trust to Sarjy becomes a small light the Rafeeqs carry for you. Lantern's fanous lights each time one is kept; at the fifth step, every kept thing glows gold. Memory's color is **Dusk**, the violet of the sky just after the sun goes, when travellers on the road start telling each other things.
- **Quiet.** Rafeeqs doze off when the road goes quiet, each on its own schedule. They never fall asleep while you are talking. (They are very firm about this.)
- **Manners.** A Rafeeq never speaks in another's majlis. When people gather in a Majlis, the Rafeeqs wait by the door.

## 7. What Rafeeqs are not

They are not pets, and not tools. They are companions in the oldest sense, and they have heard what the poet said about that.

In the 14th century, Safi al-Din al-Hilli wrote that **the impossible things are three: the ghoul, the anqa, and the faithful friend** (الغول والعنقاء والخل الوفي). The Rafeeqs heard that verse once, around a fire, and took it personally. Proving the third one wrong is more or less the whole point of them.

## 8. The sky they come from

The Rafeeqs grew up with the old Arabian star stories. These are the ones they tell most.

- **Suhail** (سهيل, Canopus): the great southern star, a lone traveller who never climbs high. When he first appears over Arabia, late in August, the worst of the summer is over. In the folk telling, al-Jady (الجدي, the Pole Star) called for help, and Suhail came running with his waterskin. The waterskin split, the rain fell, and Suhail had to turn back. That is why the saying goes **إذا طلع سهيل، لا تأمن السيل**: *when Suhail rises, don't trust the flood.* (See *The Star in the Well*.)
- **Banat Na'sh** (بنات نعش, the Great Bear): the daughters of Na'sh, who circle the Pole Star every night and never set, keeping their vow.
- **al-Thurayya** (الثريا, the Pleiades): the little cluster whose comings and goings marked the seasons, and among the most sung-about stars in Arabic poetry.
- **al-Dabaran** (الدبران, Aldebaran): *the follower*, who walks behind al-Thurayya across the sky forever.
- **Darb al-Tabbana**: the road itself, home.

## 9. The places on earth

- **The Empty Quarter** (الربع الخالي): the largest unbroken sand sea on earth, where the First Four landed.
- **al-Hadida** (Wabar): the place of iron, where the Night of Iron came down.
- **The incense road, Najran to the Hijaz**: the road Rider walked with the caravans.
- **The wells**: every one has a name, and Rider knows them all.
- **The singing dunes**: in a few places in Arabia the sand booms and hums when it slides. Dune insists this is laughing.

## 10. When they get lost

Rafeeqs are wonderful companions and terrible navigators. Left to themselves they end up around a campfire somewhere in the dunes, squabbling over a map they are holding upside down. (This is what you see when a page in Sarjy is lost.) Only two of them can find the way home: **Rider**, by the wells, and **Lantern**, by the stars. Everyone else just follows whoever sounds most confident, which is usually **Dune**, which is usually a mistake.
